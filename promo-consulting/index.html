You asked: is code inexpensive while reasoning incurs AI fees? Trying to learn the economics.

Answered Exactly right, and it is why the platform is built the way it is.

Deterministic code costs effectively nothing per run. Parsing a file, matching records, doing the margin maths, applying a threshold, building a workbook: that is ordinary software, and running it a thousand times costs about the same as running it once.

Model reasoning is metered per run. Reading a screen, deciding which tool to use, handling an exception, writing a summary: each of those is a billable API call.

So every calculation we move from the model into code makes the run both more accurate and cheaper. That is why we insist that anything producing a number is code. It is a correctness decision first and a cost decision second, and the two happen to align.

You asked: how do we stop users running up huge usage charges that disrupt our pricing model?

Answered Under the structure above, you cannot be disrupted by it, because AltOps absorbs the model cost, not PC and not the client. Runaway usage is our problem to manage, and our margin at risk rather than yours. That is deliberate: it gives us the incentive to keep the platform efficient, and it gives you a predictable number to price against.

Mechanically we meter usage per client and per run, so we see cost per client in real time, and we can set caps and alerts on any client that behaves unusually.

You asked: explain scale-to-zero in plain English. I want to keep costs down and margins up for both of us.

Answered Each person using the platform has their own machine in the cloud. Today those machines stay switched on whether or not anyone is using them, which means we pay for idle time.

Scale-to-zero switches a machine off when nobody is working and switches it back on when a task arrives, with the browser sessions and logins still intact so nothing has to be set up again. The user cannot tell the difference apart from a few seconds on the first task.

Why it matters to you: it is the difference between infrastructure cost that grows with every client and infrastructure cost that grows with actual usage. It is the single biggest lever on the cost of serving a client, which is what makes the pricing above sustainable as PC scales.

04

What the platform can do

You asked: where do AI learning, reasoning and adaptation come in? Can it suggest and request human approval, while not deciding on its own outside established guardrails?

Answered That is precisely the design. A recorded workflow is the deterministic spine, so the routine path is repeatable and cannot drift. The model handles what the recording did not anticipate: reading an unexpected screen, deciding which tool fits, handling an exception, writing the summary.

The guardrails are explicit written rules, not model judgment, and they are yours to edit. Where a decision is not the agent's to make, it pauses, notifies the responsible person, and waits. The person's reply steers what happens next.

You asked: can a human engage with the supervised browser, give approvals and choose next steps, or is it just informational?

Answered Fully interactive. You can watch a run live, take over the browser mid-run, do the step yourself, and hand control back. The agent picks up from where you left it with the context of what you did.

You asked: can agents have their own identity and log in to Syncore as their own user, acting like an employee on a schedule, reporting to a human, with their own audit trail?

Answered Yes, and we would recommend it. An agent is set up as its own worker with its own credentials, its own isolated machine and its own login to the vendor system. Actions appear in Syncore's audit log under that identity rather than a person's, which is cleaner for accountability and for your clients' auditors.

It runs on a schedule, reports to a named human, and escalates to them when a decision is not its to make. That is the model we would build toward for a distributor's back office.

One agent login per client is enough. Every agent running under it is identified individually in the platform's own record, so you can show exactly which agent did what.

You asked: logins. You do not want a person involved on every run. Does the client have to give AltOps their credentials?

Answered No. The client logs in to Syncore, QuickBooks, their bank and the rest inside the AltOps browser themselves. We never see the credentials. Sessions persist. About once every thirty days one person logs back in. That is the only touch.

You asked: what if we want to replace the work of three A/P staff but need it done in one day? How does it work faster than human pace, and can non-urgent work run at night?

Answered Two answers.

  • Run many at once. The work is split across multiple agents running in parallel, each with its own browser session. Three people's daily volume is a matter of how many run concurrently, not how fast one agent types.
  • The recorded path is fast. Once a workflow is recorded, replay is direct rather than the agent reasoning about each screen, so it is both quicker and cheaper than a fully reasoned run.

And yes, scheduling is live today. Non-urgent work runs overnight on a clock with nobody present, which is both easier on the vendor systems and cheaper to run.

You asked: Trillium is calling on the same clients. What is the difference?

Answered Trillium is built on APIs. We use APIs where they exist and drive the screen where they do not. That is the whole difference, and it matters more than it sounds.

Syncore's API does not cover most of what a back office does. Adding artwork, working from a spreadsheet, reading a product image to build a quote: none of that has an endpoint. An API-only tool cannot do it. We can. We also use Syncore's own internal calls where its public API stops, which is faster than the screen.

The other difference is cost. The first few runs of a workflow are reasoned by the model and cost real money, say $10. The platform records the path, and by the fourth run it replays for cents. Cheaper, and the same every time, which is what you want for books.

Both companies are funded, them by Y Combinator, us by Mucker Capital. Neither is going away. Compare the platforms, not the balance sheets. A distributor on shopVOX recently moved from Trillium to us because their quoting depended on reading images, and we can arrange a reference.

You asked: is the recorded workflow process proprietary? Why build your own rather than use something like Claude's Record a Skill? Could we use that and import into AltOps?

Answered It is ours, and it does something different. A skill file is written instructions: helpful, but the model still decides how to carry them out, which means it can vary between runs. Our recorder captures the actual identity of each element a person touched and produces a program that replays exactly, with the model stepping in only when a step no longer matches.

For financial back-office work that difference matters, because the same input should produce the same actions every time. On importing: written instructions and playbooks can be brought in as instruction presets today, and we would happily accept skill files that way.

You asked: what about testing? First run on the live platform, or sandbox first? Won't distributor owners worry about non-expert staff building agents that do the wrong thing?

Answered That worry is correct, and it is the reason for the review step.

A recorded workflow does not go straight to unattended production. It is run under supervision against real data first, its output reconciled against the source system, and only then trusted to run on its own. The platform also has an exploration mode that can read a system without taking any irreversible action, which is how we map an unfamiliar setup safely.

Once PC has a certified reviewer, that gate sits inside PC. Nothing a staff member records goes live to a client unreviewed.

You asked: what exactly does "we do not connect directly into a client's production database" mean?

Answered It means we never take a direct database connection into a client's accounting or ERP system and read or write its tables underneath the application.

We work the way a person or an approved integration does: through the vendor's API, or through the application's own interface. The client's own permissions, validation rules and audit trail all still apply. It is a deliberate constraint. Writing straight into a production database is how integrations silently corrupt data.

You asked: multiple tabs per user with multiple logins? Does the user set this up for every run? Can it be automated and run in the cloud?

Answered It is automated and it runs in the cloud. Logins are set up once and persist, so nobody re-authenticates per run. When several agents run at once, each gets its own browser session sharing that stored login. Nobody opens tabs by hand, and nothing depends on anyone's laptop being awake.

You asked: how long are logs kept? A vendor invoice copy may need seven years under IRS audit rules.

Answered Retention is configurable per client, and seven years is supportable. We would set financial evidence, meaning the documents and screenshots behind a booked figure, to the retention period the client's own policy requires, and write that into the agreement. Worth confirming what your clients' policies specify so we set the default correctly.

You noted: most bookkeepers cannot show line by line where a figure came from, which is a reason AI is better for bookkeeping.

Answered Agreed, and we think it is one of the strongest things you can sell. Every figure carries its source records, the screens it was read from, the rule applied and the version of the instructions in force. It is a stronger audit trail than most manual processes produce, and it does not depend on anyone remembering what they did in March.

05

Running it at scale

You asked: when Syncore makes a material change and most agents stop working, how is that fixed at scale? What if there are 100 agents across 100 clients?

Answered The answer depends on agents sharing definitions rather than each being bespoke, which is how we build them.

  • It surfaces immediately. A changed screen makes runs fail loudly rather than quietly producing wrong output, so we know within one run cycle, not at month end.
  • Many steps heal themselves. Recorded steps carry several ways to identify an element, and the agent falls back to reasoning when a step no longer matches, so a cosmetic change often does not break anything.
  • One fix propagates. Where a hundred agents share a Syncore workflow definition, the repair is made once and applies to all of them.
  • It is our cost. Repairs are included, not billed as change requests.

We will be straight about the exposure: a deep change to a core Syncore screen means a day or two of disruption on affected workflows while we repair and re-verify. The manual fallback covers that window, which brings us to your next point.

You asked: if AI leads to staff reductions, an extended outage creates a backlog and regression to manual may not be feasible. What recovery commitment goes in writing?

For the call This is the most serious operational risk in the model and you are right to raise it. Once a client has restructured around the agents, "just do it manually" stops being a real answer.

What reduces it: work is queued rather than lost, so an outage delays rather than destroys, and the queue is drained at multiple times human speed when service resumes. Failures are usually specific to one workflow rather than platform-wide. And model failover is built in: Anthropic is the primary model, and if it errors, runs fall back to OpenAI or Google automatically. All three are under terms that prohibit training on your clients' data.

On the call you made the useful distinction. Forty-eight hours is survivable for month-end finance. Purchase orders and CSR work are not. We would write recovery targets into the agreement by workflow type rather than one blanket number, and set them with you.

You asked: examples and references for private or on-premise deployment. Benefit versus AltOps hosting, cost, and should PC do this for itself?

For the call Our honest recommendation is that PC should not, at least not at first.

Private deployment exists for the client who contractually cannot use shared infrastructure. The benefit is that their data never touches ours. The cost is that they give up the operational advantages of hosting: updates, monitoring, capacity and recovery all become slower, and someone has to own the environment.

For PC, hosted means we carry the infrastructure, the cost and the responsibility. Taking it in-house moves all three to you, for a security benefit you already get from tenant isolation. We would keep private deployment as the answer for the occasional client who demands it, and price it separately when that comes up. Happy to walk through the setup in detail on a call.

You asked: examples of recovery fixes and how many incidents?

Answered The recoveries we have run in practice are worker and browser-level: a crashed session restored with logins intact, a stuck run reclaimed and resumed by another worker, a bad deployment rolled back. We have not had to rebuild the platform from backups, so the full-restore path is tested rather than battle-proven, and we would rather say that than imply otherwise. Formalising and drilling it is part of the SOC 2 work.

06

Security, knowledge and compliance

You asked: differentiate between "data" being used to train models and "process or knowledge" being used. Explain in practical terms.

Answered Three different things get confused here, so it is worth separating them.

  • Client data is invoices, job numbers, customer names, figures. It is sent to the model as the working context of a single run, the model answers, and it is gone. It is not retained to train anything, by us or by the model provider, and it never reaches another client.
  • Process knowledge is a written rule, such as "freight over 10% of quote gets flagged to the client." That is not data, it is know-how. It is stored as a record you can read, edit and export.
  • Model training means changing a model's weights using your material. We do not do it, and our providers do not do it with commercial API traffic.

In practice: nothing your clients type ends up making anyone else's AI smarter. Knowledge only moves if a person decides to move it, which is your next question.

You asked: could a client's knowledge, not their data, be made available to PC so you can improve processes and agents, with the client's permission? And what development is needed to make it ready?

Answered Yes, and it is a good instinct. It is technically supported and it is the right shape for a partner.

Knowledge is already stored as discrete records, each carrying the rule, why it exists and where it came from, which makes it possible to promote one without dragging any client data along. With the client's permission, PC can promote a rule from a client tenant into the PC library, where it is reviewed by your SME and, once approved, inherited by other PC clients.

Three properties we would keep: promotion is always a human decision rather than automatic, it needs client consent, and client-identifying content is stripped on the way up. That gives PC a library that compounds across your portfolio while no client's information reaches another.

On what needs building: the core exists today. When we build an agent from a recording, the platform writes a structured SOP of how that client does the work, with no data in it. What we would add for PC is the layer you described on the call: your benchmarks as the starting point, a comparison of any requested change against the benchmark and against other PC clients, a recommendation with the reasoning, and escalation to the owner when PC advises against a change. PC decides every time. Nothing is automatic. We will help design it, and the benchmarks and outputs are your knowledge.

You asked: can an agent or a group of agents have its own pod and act as an employee while reporting to a human?

Answered Yes. That is exactly the model: each worker gets its own isolated machine and storage, its own credentials and its own task queue, and reports to a named person who sees its work and handles what it escalates.

You asked: a realistic SOC 2 date, and whether Type I lets us say "SOC 2 compliant".

Answered Type I in roughly six to eight weeks, on Thoropass's current expectation. Type II a month to six weeks after that. So full SOC 2 Type II before the end of the year. We are a small codebase with few people and devices to audit, which is why it is quick.

On wording: once Type I is issued we will say SOC 2 Type I, and the badge says so. We will not call Type I Type II, and we would ask PC to hold the same line. The moment Thoropass fixes the dates we send them to you.

Answered Not as a polished sales asset yet, and it is a good idea. We will also complete PC's vendor security questionnaire, and we can answer client questionnaires directly on your behalf during an RFP.
